About

The dog trainer behind the academy.

Ryan R. McKenzie trains dogs and coaches their families from Meteghan, on Nova Scotia’s French Shore. Acadia Canine Academy was built on one principle: dogs learn best when their handlers are consistent, clear, and committed.

Our Approach

Built from experience. Backed by results.

Based in Meteghan on Nova Scotia’s French Shore, we train on an outdoor training field on our 3-acre property, with an indoor training space coming soon. We emphasize clear communication and consistent technique. Training is a discipline, not a shortcut, aimed at calm, focused, reliable dogs.

From foundational obedience to behaviour modification and rehabilitation for reactive and high-needs dogs, every program is rooted in clarity, fairness, and structure.

Our Philosophy on Tools

A tool is only as good as the hands holding it.

Leashes, long lines, treats, toys and e-collars are all just tools. What matters is how they are used, and whether the dog understands what is being asked.

Good training draws on all four quadrants of how dogs learn: adding something good, taking away something unpleasant, adding something the dog doesn't want, and taking away something the dog does want. We are a balanced program, and we would rather be upfront about that. Every program we run uses all four, even simple leash walking on a flat collar, because gentle leash pressure is something a dog learns to switch off by choosing the right spot. Rewards, play and your relationship with your dog do most of the work. Any correction is fair, low and clearly taught first, so your dog always knows how to get it right.

We think of a modern e-collar as a communication tool, never a way to hurt or frighten a dog. Every dog starts by simply wearing the collar during meals, play and training until it feels normal. Only then do we look for the dog's working level: the lowest setting where the dog shows a small sign of noticing, like an ear flick or a turn of the head. On a 0 to 100 dial, most dogs in a quiet setting work somewhere from the low single digits into the teens. It varies by dog, by collar fit and by how exciting the surroundings are, so we watch the dog, not the number. Ryan tests every collar on himself first.

At that level the sensation is more like a tap on the shoulder than anything sharp. We pair it with commands the dog already knows, the tap stops the moment the dog responds, and the response is always rewarded. Over time the dog learns that the collar simply means "listen, there's something good in this for you."

The collar is never used to zap a dog for ordinary misbehaving.

We also want to be open about rehabilitation. Some dogs come to us with serious, long-standing behaviour problems, and for a few of them there is still a place for higher levels, used carefully and briefly as part of a full behaviour modification plan. For some severe cases, the honest choice families face is behaviour modification that includes an e-collar, or euthanasia. We would always rather give that dog a real chance at a safe, happy life.

In untrained hands, however good the intentions, an e-collar can hurt a dog physically and mentally. That is why we ask clients to keep their own e-collar work to the low, reward-paired level we teach, and only once we have trained them to use it properly.

Quality matters too. The brands we trust and use are E-Collar Technologies (the Educator line), our first choice for its fine 0 to 100 control and smooth, gentle stimulation, and Dogtra, our other recommendation. Cheap collars often have uneven output and big jumps between levels, which makes low-level work much harder.

For comparison, many trainers once recommended the rattle can: a soda can filled with pennies or stones, shaken or even thrown at a dog to startle it away from the counter or the garbage. Few people thought twice about it. A well-conditioned e-collar at a low level is far quieter and more precise than that, which is why we believe many calls to ban e-collars come from a misunderstanding of how modern trainers actually use them. Many of the same people calling to ban e-collars would be shocked to learn that a can of stones thrown at a dog was once everyday advice.

In the end, it all comes back to the dog. A well-trained dog lives a fuller, happier life because of the freedom training brings: more time off leash, more trust from the people around them, and more places they can go with their family. Used correctly, the e-collar is one of the best tools we know of for getting them there.
