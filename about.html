Our Approach

Built from experience. Backed by results.

Based in Meteghan on Nova Scotia’s French Shore, we train on an outdoor training field on our 3-acre property, with an indoor training space coming soon. We emphasize clear communication and consistent technique. Training is a discipline, not a shortcut, aimed at calm, focused, reliable dogs.

Our Philosophy on Tools

A tool is only as good as the hands holding it.

Leashes, long lines, treats, toys and e-collars are all just tools. What matters is how they are used, and whether the dog understands what is being asked.

We think of a modern e-collar as a communication tool, never a way to hurt or frighten a dog. We work at the working level, the lowest level the dog notices and responds to, pair it with commands the dog already knows, and always follow with a reward. Ryan tests every collar on himself first.

Every tool is introduced slowly, used fairly and kept in proportion to the dog in front of us. Read the full story in Our Approach to Training Tools.

Our Training Philosophy

How we think about teaching dogs.

Balanced training and force-free training

We have a lot of respect for force-free trainers. Rewards are how we teach too, and for showing a dog what we want, food, toys and praise are hard to beat.

Where the two approaches part ways is reliability. Pet dogs live in the real world, with traffic, livestock, deer and porcupines. A recall that works most of the time is not good enough when the dog is heading for the highway. Force-free training stops short here, because any pressure at all, even a light tug on a leash, gets counted as force and ruled out.

So we teach with rewards first. Once the dog truly understands a behaviour, we add fair, clear pressure and correction it has already learned how to turn off. That is what keeps a dog safe when it matters most, and a safe dog gets far more freedom. Read more in our article on choosing a trainer.

All four quadrants, used fairly

Dogs learn from consequences, and scientists sort those consequences into four groups. We use all of them, in proportion, and we would rather explain them than hide them.

Positive reinforcement: adding something the dog likes to make a behaviour happen more. Your dog sits, you say "yes" and hand over a treat.

Negative reinforcement: taking away something mildly unpleasant when the dog does the right thing. Light leash pressure eases off the moment your dog steps back beside you.

Positive punishment: adding something the dog does not want to make a behaviour happen less. A quick, fair leash pop when a dog that knows "down" pops straight back up.

Negative punishment: taking away something the dog wants. Your dog jumps on you, so you turn and walk away and the attention stops.

Rewards do most of the work. Corrections come only once the dog understands, and they are always matched to the dog and the situation. For more, read our four quadrants article.

Classical conditioning: what goes with what

Long before a dog learns what to do, it learns what goes with what. That is classical conditioning, first studied by Ivan Pavlov, and it runs through every session we teach.

Our marker word "yes" means nothing to a puppy until it has come right before a treat over and over. Then the word itself makes the dog light up. The e-collar works the same way. At the working level, the lowest level the dog notices and responds to, it is paired with commands the dog knows and with rewards, so it comes to mean "listen, something good is coming." The leash comes to mean a walk, and a crate paired with meals, chews and quiet comes to mean calm.

It works the other way too. A crate used only as punishment, or a car ride that always ends at the vet, builds the wrong link. We are careful about what we pair with what. You can read more in Pavlov's Dogs: What Classical Conditioning Means for Your Dog.

Clarity over volume

Dogs do not understand louder. They understand clear. A command given once, in a normal voice, and followed through every time teaches more than the same word shouted five times.

That is why we keep our words few and consistent. "Yes" marks the right choice, "okay" releases the dog, and "no" tells the dog it made a wrong choice. Owners are welcome to pick their own words, as long as everyone in the house uses the same ones every time. For why this works, read How Dogs Think, Learn and Communicate. The same goes for how we talk about bites. We use a shared, objective scale so everyone means the same thing, explained in Understanding the Dunbar Bite Scale.
