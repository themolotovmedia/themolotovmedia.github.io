What to Do Before You Use an E-Collar

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

A lot of people pick up an e-collar hoping it will fix pulling, a weak recall or a dog that explodes on leash. The collar can become a clear, kind way to talk to a dog at a distance. It will not do that job by itself.

The e-collar does not train the dog. Training trains the dog. If the foundation is missing, a remote in your hand often makes the mess bigger, not smaller. Before we use the collar as a working tool, we want five things in place.

This is separate from teaching your dog to feel good about the collar itself. We introduce the Mini Educator by conditioning, the same way we charge the word “yes”: the signal comes first, and something good follows. That work is in our free guide, E-Collar Introduction, and we explain the idea behind it in our article on Pavlov and classical conditioning. The list below is about what should already be true before you ask the collar to run real training.

1. Learn the leash first

A leash is not only what stops your dog from leaving. It is how you talk with your hands.

We use leash pressure as guidance: a low, steady feel on the collar that shows your dog the way, and turns off the instant your dog moves with you. A bump is the in-between: a series of soft pulsing tugs on the collar used for direction, not a correction. A leash pop is a correction, a single crisp tug, and we use it only when the work calls for it. A bump is not the same as steady leash pressure.

Start with loose-leash walking. Once the leash can stay slack, you can use it to show your dog what to start, what to keep doing and what to stop. That matters later, because the e-collar is pressure without a direction your dog can see. The leash has a line and a handler at the other end. When we layer the Mini Educator over a skill, we still use the leash to help the feel make sense. If you cannot yet talk to your dog on a leash, a number on a remote will not do that talking for you.

We walk through the basics in Loose-Leash Walking: The Basics That Actually Work.

2. Teach the everyday behaviours on a leash

Before the collar runs a skill, your dog should already know that skill. On one cue, on a leash, in a quiet place, without food sitting in your hand, we want:

  • Sit, down and stand, each held until you say “okay”
  • Place
  • Heel, at least for a few steps
  • Come

We do not teach a separate stay command. Each position includes the hold. We build duration by waiting a little longer before “yes”, and we use “good” as a bridge early on.

Teaching those behaviours is how you learn the rest of the job: charging a marker, timing, how to motivate your dog, how to reward, and how pressure and release work. If those pieces are fuzzy on a leash, they will still be fuzzy when the leash is gone. The collar never teaches something new. It only lays a wireless leash over work your dog already understands. Those everyday behaviours are what we teach in Functional Obedience.

3. Give a young puppy time to be a puppy

There is no fixed age for introducing the collar itself. A puppy can go through the conditioning in E-Collar Introduction when it is ready: the collar comes out and good things happen, it learns to wear the Mini Educator like any other collar, we find the lowest level it can feel, and we pair that light touch with food until the touch is good news. Those low levels are not painful. We never use the collar to correct a puppy.

Using the collar as a working tool is a later step. For most pet owners, a sensible point to start that later work is when the dog is around six months old and the basics above are in place. That is a guideline, not a law. Go by your dog, not the calendar.

The early months are for teaching your puppy that learning with you is fun. Charge “yes”. Teach crate manners, play, an out from tug, and simple structure in the house. Practise the basics on a leash. Get out into the world together. You can add more control later. You do not get those first months back.

4. Use a proper Mini Educator

The remote we use, and the one our guides are written around, is the E-Collar Technologies Mini Educator ET-300, the round yellow remote. The dial runs from 0 to 100, so you can find a working level, the lowest level your dog notices and responds to, and move one small step at a time. We check that level at the start of every session, because it changes with excitement, weather and how the collar sits.

A cheap unit with big jumps between levels makes it much harder to be fair. Fine steps let you watch the dog, not guess at a coarse number. If you want to see the collar we use, visit our E-Collar Technologies page. We are an authorized dealer.

5. Have a plan, and follow it

E-collar training is not pressing a button when your dog does not listen. If it were that simple, every dog on a remote would already be reliable.

Arrive with a plan and stay with it. Our plan looks like this:

  • Condition the collar first, the way we describe in E-Collar Introduction (free when you join our mailing list).
  • Teach the leash and the everyday behaviours.
  • Find the working level, and pair the touch with skills your dog already knows.
  • Only then think about distance, distraction and, much later, a fair correction on a behaviour the dog knows fluently. Corrections wait. They are never for puppies, and they are never for a skill the dog has not been taught.

The full how-to is in our E-Collar Training Guide. You can also start with a free walk-in consultation in Meteghan, or an in-home visit anywhere in the Tri-County area (Digby, Clare and Yarmouth).

A simple way to remember it

If you cannot yet walk on a loose leash, mark with “yes”, and get sit, down, stand, place, heel and come on one cue, you are not behind. You are still in the part that matters most. Do that work first. Introduce the Mini Educator so your dog loves wearing it and turns to you at a light touch. Then, and only then, let the collar become a wireless leash.
