Functional Obedience and a Daily Training Schedule

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

When people say they want a trained dog, they usually mean the same handful of things. They want a dog that does not drag them down the street. They want sit and down when they ask, and they want those positions to hold until they say otherwise. They want a dog that will go to place and stay there, come when called, walk next to them when asked, and go into a crate without turning the house upside down. That set of skills is functional obedience.

It is not sport work. Sport can take years of short, careful sessions, and that is a different project. Functional obedience is the manners that make daily life safer and calmer. If your dog also has behaviour problems such as fear, reactivity or guarding, those need their own plan. Clear obedience helps a great deal, but it is not a substitute for behaviour modification.

It does not take years. It does take a rhythm.

For a typical pet dog, functional obedience is not a years-long project if you work clearly and often. It also does not happen because you booked a lesson and waited. Short, honest sessions most days do more than an occasional marathon. Hard cases take longer. Weekly lessons only work if the homework happens between them.

Our Functional Obedience course is 10 sessions with you and your dog. We teach the skills, the cues, a daily training rhythm and how to proof that work in real life. That is not the same as weeks of daily professional handling. We will not claim those results from an owner course. What you practise at home is a core part of the training.

What we are teaching

In our language, each position already includes the hold. We do not teach a separate stay command. Sit means sit until we release with "okay". The same is true for down, stand, place and heel. We mark the moment a behaviour is right with "yes". While a dog is holding a position, "good" can keep them there. "Okay" ends the picture and does not imply a reward by itself.

  • Loose-leash walking, so the leash hangs soft instead of turning every outing into a tug of war
  • Sit, down, stand, place and heel, each held until "okay"
  • Come, taught first on a long line so the dog cannot practise ignoring you
  • Crate, as its own cue: go in, settle, and stay quietly until released
  • Leave it, doorway manners and calm greetings with four paws on the floor

Place is one of the most useful pieces of this picture. If you want the teaching steps, see Why Place Training Matters for Every Dog. Crate work has its own article too: Why Every Dog Should Be Crate Trained. Loose-leash walking is covered in Loose-Leash Walking: The Basics That Actually Work.

A schedule you can keep

Dogs do not learn well in one long grind. Teach, then let the dog rest and soak up the lesson. Come back later for another short set. An hour of stacked new pictures is too much for most dogs, and it is far too much for a puppy.

Use meals. Do not free-feed unless your veterinarian has you on a different plan. Most dogs eat twice a day, and those two meals are two ready-made sessions. Instead of setting the bowl down for free, run the food through your hands as payment for short work.

Puppies, until about six months

Treat a dog this age as a puppy. Attention is short. Expectations should be lower than they will be later. You can push a puppy toward older-dog standards, but it is kinder and more effective to let puppies be puppies while you still teach the skills that matter.

Two sessions a day is enough, and mealtimes are the natural place for them. Keep those sessions short. Ten minutes is plenty. Fifteen minutes is a long session for many puppies. If the puppy gets silly, frustrated or tired, you have already gone too long. End while they still want more.

If you do this every day, your puppy will know a lot by six months. That does not mean adult reliability. It means a strong start and a good feeling about training, so you can raise the standard later. A great deal of puppy work is also about showing them the world carefully: people, surfaces, sounds and places, kept under threshold.

Our Puppy Foundations course is built for that early window. Functional Obedience is the next step for adolescent and adult dogs, and for families ready to turn those foundations into everyday manners.

Adolescent and adult dogs

The same idea holds, with a higher standard. Several short sessions across the day beat one long one. If you only have two windows, use the meals. Add a little formal work to real walks: a few steps of quality loose-leash walking, a sit before you clip the leash, place while you eat, a paid recall on a long line in a quiet place.

You do not need to train every skill every day. Touch the important pictures across the week: attention, positions, recall, walking, leave it, place and real-life manners at doors and greetings.

Teach one thing, then keep the standard

There is a difference between teaching a new behaviour and maintaining one the dog already understands. Do not try to install recall, sit, down, place and heel as brand-new lessons all at once.

Start with one picture. A common first everyday standard is loose-leash walking. When the dog generally understands not to pull, that becomes a rule you still keep while you teach sit and down. You have not abandoned the walk. You have moved it from new lesson to living standard. The same pattern follows for come, place and the rest.

Functional obedience is everyday life, not a class you attend and then forget. Short lessons teach the new thing. The rest of the day maintains what the dog already understands.

Two mistakes that stall the work

The first is treating obedience as something that only happens in the kitchen, the backyard or a training room. A sit that exists only on a quiet mat will not hold up on a sidewalk.

The second is the opposite extreme: cue after cue, with the dog never allowed to simply exist near you. That creates a drill-sergeant relationship. Some dogs perform while you are issuing orders and fall apart the moment you stop. Others shut down.

Neither extreme is useful. Show the dog the basic standards for life, then give those standards room to live inside an ordinary day.

The point is freedom

The point of functional obedience is freedom. We want a dog that can meander and sniff around us, then come when we say come, walk next to us when we say heel, and lie down when we ask, even if they were having fun a moment ago. Then we say okay and give them their life back.

A well-trained pet dog is not a robot, and it is not an untrained companion we hope will guess correctly. It is a dog that can be loose around us because we both know the words still work.
