Helping a Reactive Dog: Start With the Foundation

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

If your dog barks and lunges at other dogs on a walk, the natural urge is to go back out tomorrow and try to win that fight. We understand why. The walk is where the problem shows up, so it feels like the walk is where the work should happen.

That is usually the wrong place to start. A dog that is already over threshold on a busy sidewalk is not in a state to learn a new choice. You are late to the moment, the leash is tight, and both of you are rehearsing the same explosion. The aim, for most families, is a dog that can walk past other dogs and leave them alone. Getting there starts well before the next surprise on the road.

We reset the foundation first. That means a shared activity your dog actually looks forward to with you, a day your dog can predict, the basic manners and house rules you can already keep in calmer moments, and only then planned sessions around other dogs.

Give your dog a reason to work with you

Start by learning what lights your dog up. If nothing is obvious, build a daily activity the two of you share: a game, a walk you both enjoy, a bit of training that ends well. The point is to get on the same team. If every interaction is about fixing a problem, progress gets much harder.

Come back to why you got the dog in the first place. Walks, hikes, a running partner, time outside together. That is the long-term goal, not winning an argument on the sidewalk.

Give the day a shape your dog can guess

A reactive dog living in chaos has a harder time. When wake-up, walks, rest, play and meals land at random, arousal spills from one part of the day into the next.

Fit the routine to your real household, not an ideal one. For many dogs, two walks and one short play session, with you present and working on something, already give the day a clear shape. Do not wander along on your phone with no plan. Add a place session around leaving and coming home, so departures and arrivals are not a scramble. We walk through that skill in Why Place Training Matters for Every Dog.

The routine has to live inside your actual schedule. The dog cannot train around a life you do not lead.

Teach the basics in calm settings first

Before we ask a dog to make a good choice around another dog, we want the conversation to already work in easier places. In our language that means:

  • Loose-leash walking
  • Sit and down, each held until we say "okay"
  • Come
  • Crate
  • Place
  • Ordinary house rules you can keep

We do not teach a separate stay command. Each position includes the hold. We mark a completed correct behaviour with "yes", then pay it. While the dog is holding a position, "good" can keep them there. "Okay" is the release. If the dog offers something we did not ask for, "wrong" is our no-reward word. "No" is the correction, used when a rule has been broken.

This stage is as much for you as for the dog. You learn how to teach, how to reward, how your dog learns, and how to read what your dog offers back. Those skills are the ones you will need later, when another dog is in the picture.

Loose-leash walking is covered in Loose-Leash Walking: The Basics That Actually Work. Crate work has its own article: Why Every Dog Should Be Crate Trained. Place is explained in Why Place Training Matters for Every Dog. The set of everyday manners is what we teach in Functional Obedience.

Practise keeping rules where the stakes are lower

Physical thresholds are a useful early classroom: the front door, the car door, the crate door, the couch. The dog waits to be invited, you mark the hold with "good" if you need to keep them there, and you release with "okay".

These waits are not a magic cure for reactivity. They exist so you learn to set a rule and keep it, and so your dog learns that a rule means something, before the stakes involve another dog.

The same idea applies to smaller unwanted habits: stealing shoes, raiding counters, mouthing when excited. The first conversation may not be taken seriously. Show that you mean it, then get back to the rest of the day.

You are ready to move on when two things are true. Your dog is looking forward to working and going out with you, and your dog already accepts ordinary household rules.

Then set up planned sessions around other dogs

Once the foundation is in place, we can address the reactivity itself. We keep three expectations clear:

  • No barking or lunging at other dogs
  • No approaching other dogs without permission
  • The dog still has to listen when another dog is around

Do not try to fix this ad hoc around the neighbourhood, hoping the right dog appears at the right distance. Set up dedicated sessions you can actually think through.

Choose a setup that matches what you and your dog can handle. A known, calm helper dog in a quiet place is the easiest start. Harder pictures come later: a dog behind a fence, the outside of a busy park, and so on. Arrive with a plan. If the dog does this, you do that. Warm up first, the same way you would before any other session. A handler who only reacts after the blow-up is always a step behind.

Give the dog freedom of response. Work on a loose leash. Do not pin the dog in sit, down or heel for the whole session so the real choice never shows up. The job in that session is to change what the dog does about other dogs, not to reward a sit that hides the problem. If the dog offers a position on its own, get it back to a loose leash. Formal positions still have a place in daily life and in management. They are not the same thing as changing the choice about the trigger.

Then attach a clear consequence to the choice. A good choice, looking away, sniffing, moving on, checking in with you, gets "yes" and is paid. A bad choice, barking, lunging, trying to drag you in, gets "no" right then. If you correct on a leash, that correction is a leash pop: a single crisp tug. That is not the same as leash pressure, which is low, constant guidance, or a bump, which is a series of soft pulsing tugs used for direction.

After the old option is closed, let the dog pick a new one. Sniffing, using the environment, looking to you to play or work: do not micromanage a specific substitute. Then take your dog's feedback. If you meant to stop the outburst and the dog keeps doing it, the consequence was not effective. That is a good moment to get coaching, because it is hard to see what your own dog is offering when you are in the middle of it.

Other dogs are a competing reinforcer. Food, toys and your attention are competing with something the dog already cares about. We write about that problem, with squirrels as the plain example, in When the Squirrel Wins: Competing Reinforcers in Plain English.

You are ready to move on when you know, in the moment, what to do if the dog blows up, ignores the other dog, or tries to say hello without permission.

Work out what is driving the reaction

The display can look the same from the sidewalk and come from different places.

Some dogs are afraid. Other dogs feel unsafe, and the outburst is meant to make the threat go away. Some dogs are excited or aroused. They want to get to the other dog and are held back by the leash. Many dogs, after a lot of rehearsal, are both. The display itself has become part of the outing.

The work then is to give the dog new evidence that challenges the old prediction. A fearful dog needs proof that nothing bad happens. An excited dog needs proof that seeing another dog does not mean play. A quiet parallel walk with a known playmate, with no play this time, is one example of that second lesson.

Build play and work with you so they matter more than the other dog. Then keep living ordinary days: new places, walks, play, with the same rules and obedience still applying. Daily work adds up.

One slip is not a full reset

Do not spiral over every glance, tail wag, whine or raised hackles. If the dog actually breaks a rule, mark it, correct it and keep going. The work will look a bit messy while it is heading in the right direction. One lapse does not wipe out the foundation you have built.

If your dog has a bite history

Reactivity on leash is not the same thing as a bite history, but the two can overlap. If your dog has bitten a person or another dog, or come close to it, do not try to sort that out with neighbourhood setups on your own. Get a professional assessment first, so safety, management and a realistic plan come before the training picture.

When we take a Behaviour Modification case, we ask about bite history in plain language. We use the Dunbar Bite Scale to describe what actually happened, because a snap at the air and a bite that needs stitches are not the same problem. Our article Understanding the Dunbar Bite Scale walks through each level.

Then go live the life you wanted

Your relationship with your dog comes first. Optional, careful contact with a suitable dog can help challenge a fear prediction, but do not force a social life your dog does not want, and do not demand that a naturally friendly dog pretend to be a statue. Basic rules still hold. No rushing up without permission. Know what you can change and what is just the dog.

When the skills are there, use them. Hikes, camping, the walks you wanted in the first place. Do not stay stuck in fear of the last bad walk. We are also not promising a quick off-leash fix around other dogs. Off-leash freedom is a later privilege, earned after the dog can already make the right choice on a leash.

Keep reading

For the walking piece of the foundation, start with Loose-Leash Walking: The Basics That Actually Work. For settling in the house, see Why Place Training Matters for Every Dog and Why Every Dog Should Be Crate Trained.
