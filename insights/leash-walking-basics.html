Loose-Leash Walking: The Basics That Actually Work

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

Most dogs pull on the leash for a simple reason: pulling works. Every time a dog leans into the collar and the person follows, the dog learns that a tight leash gets it where it wants to go. The good news is that the same logic works in reverse. When a loose leash becomes the only way forward, dogs figure it out faster than most owners expect.

Here is how we approach loose-leash walking at Acadia Canine Academy, and how you can start at home.

Start where it is boring

A busy road or a path full of other dogs is the hardest place to learn anything. Start in your driveway, your yard or a quiet hallway. Your dog should be able to hear you, take food and think. If your dog will not take a treat, you are too close to the excitement, so back off and try somewhere calmer.

Keep the first sessions short. Five minutes of good walking teaches more than half an hour of tug of war.

Pick a spot and pay for it

Decide where you want your dog to walk, usually beside your left leg. Every time your dog is in that spot with a loose leash, mark it with a word like "yes" and give a small treat right at your leg. Delivering the reward at your side matters. If you toss treats forward, you are paying your dog to get ahead of you.

At first you may be rewarding every step or two. As your dog starts to stay close on its own, space the rewards out. Over time the walk itself, and your attention, become the reward.

Stop when the leash goes tight

The moment the leash tightens, stop moving. Stand still and wait. Most dogs will turn back to see why the walk ended, and the leash will loosen. Mark that, reward at your leg and carry on. If your dog keeps pulling, turn around and walk the other way, then reward when it catches up beside you.

This is where a lot of people give up, because the first few walks can feel like more stopping than walking. Stick with it. Your dog is learning that a tight leash stops everything and a loose leash keeps the walk going.

How gentle leash pressure fits in

We are a balanced training program, and we are open about that. Even simple leash walking on a flat collar uses gentle leash pressure, because a dog can learn to switch that pressure off by moving back into the right spot. We teach this slowly. A light, steady pull on the leash shows the dog which way to go, and the pressure releases the instant the dog moves with you. It is guidance, not a yank.

What we never want is a constant tug of war. A leash that is tight all the time teaches a dog to ignore pressure, and that makes the problem worse.

Change direction and pace

Once your dog has the idea, make the walk a little more interesting. Turn often, speed up for a few steps, slow down, stop and start again. A dog that has to keep an eye on you to know where you are going is a dog that is paying attention. Reward your dog for keeping up with every change.

Add distractions slowly

When your dog walks well in a quiet place, move somewhere a little busier. Expect things to fall apart a bit each time you raise the difficulty, and lower your expectations for a few sessions. Go back to rewarding more often. A good rule is to change only one thing at a time: a new place, more distance or more distraction, but not all three together.

Common mistakes

  • Using a retractable leash while teaching. It puts constant pressure on the collar and teaches your dog that pulling earns more line.
  • Letting the dog greet every person and dog on the walk. If greetings happen whenever your dog pulls toward them, pulling is being rewarded.
  • Only practising on the walk itself. A few short sessions in the yard each day make the real walk much easier.
  • Expecting a young or excited dog to walk perfectly after a long day inside. Give your dog a chance to sniff and stretch before you ask for focus.

When to get help

If your dog lunges, barks or spins at other dogs or people on walks, that is often more than a pulling problem. Reactivity on leash needs a plan that looks at why the dog is reacting, not just where it is walking. Our Rehabilitation program is built for those dogs.
