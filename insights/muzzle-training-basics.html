Muzzle Training: Why Every Dog Should Learn to Love a Muzzle

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

Most people only think about a muzzle when something has already gone wrong. We would like to change that. A dog that has learned to love its muzzle is safer, and it is often less stressed, because a muzzle on a dog that is used to it is just another piece of gear.

Why we teach it

  • Vet visits. Exams, vaccines and treatments can be uncomfortable. A dog that already knows its muzzle can be handled calmly, without being pinned down by strangers.
  • Grooming. Nail trims and ear cleaning go more smoothly when no one is worried about a snap.
  • Injury and emergencies. Even the gentlest dog may bite when it is badly hurt or frightened. If your dog is hit by a car or caught in a trap, the people helping it may need to muzzle it first. Some provincial emergency guides, such as Alberta’s pet preparedness checklist, include a muzzle in a pet first aid kit.
  • Behaviour work. For dogs that react or guard, a muzzle keeps everyone safe while we work on the real problem. It lets the dog and the handler relax, and relaxed dogs learn better.

Muzzle myths

Myth: Only dangerous dogs wear muzzles

A muzzle tells you that the owner is responsible, not that the dog is bad. Plenty of friendly dogs wear one for vet visits, for scavenging on walks, or simply because their family planned ahead.

Myth: A muzzle is cruel

A well-fitted basket muzzle lets a dog pant, drink and take treats. What is unkind is putting one on a dog for the first time while it is in pain or terrified. That is why we teach it early, slowly and with lots of rewards.

Myth: A muzzle fixes aggression

It does not. A muzzle manages risk. The training, the threshold work and the plan are what change behaviour, and that work belongs in our Behaviour Modification program.

Choosing a muzzle

For anything longer than a quick procedure, choose a basket muzzle, in rubber, plastic, wire or leather. Avoid tight fabric muzzles that hold the mouth closed. A dog cannot pant properly in one, so they are only meant for very short use.

  • Room to pant. Your dog should be able to open its mouth fully inside the basket.
  • Secure. The strap behind the ears should be snug enough that your dog cannot paw it off. Some muzzles add a forehead strap for extra security.
  • Comfortable. It should not rub the eyes or press on the end of the nose.

Teaching your dog to love it

The muzzle should never be forced on. We start by letting the dog sniff it, then reward any interest. Next we put treats inside the basket so the dog chooses to push its nose in. Only once that is easy do we fasten the strap, for a few seconds at first, then longer, always paired with food and fun. Before long, many dogs push their face into the muzzle when they see it come out, because it means good things are coming. If your dog backs away, slow down. We go at the dog’s pace, every time.

Optional, but highly recommended

We know some people do not like muzzles, and we respect that. That is why crate training, the muzzle introduction and the e-collar introduction are optional in our Puppy Foundations and Basic Obedience courses, and none of them is needed to graduate. Still, we highly recommend all three. Each one makes life safer and calmer for your dog when things do not go to plan.
