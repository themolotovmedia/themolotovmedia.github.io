Our Approach to Training Tools

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

Leashes, long lines, treats, toys, crates, muzzles and e-collars are all just tools. None of them trains a dog on its own. What matters is how they are used, and whether the dog understands what is being asked.

Good training draws on all four quadrants of how dogs learn: adding something good, taking away something unpleasant, adding something the dog does not want, and taking away something the dog does want. We are a balanced program, and we would rather be upfront about that. Rewards, play and your relationship with your dog do most of the work. Any correction is fair, low and clearly taught first, so your dog always knows how to get it right.

How we choose a tool

We pick the least we need to communicate clearly with the dog in front of us. A soft, eager puppy and a strong adult dog that pulls through a fence line do not need the same thing. Every tool is introduced slowly, made familiar before it is used, and taught so the dog knows how to turn any pressure off. As training improves, we aim to need less.

Flat collar

Most dogs start here. A flat collar holds ID tags and is enough for teaching early leash skills. Even simple leash walking on a flat collar uses gentle pressure, because the dog learns to switch it off by choosing the right spot beside you. We use three leash actions. Leash pressure is low, constant guidance. A bump is a series of soft pulsing tugs for direction, not a correction. A leash pop is a correction.

Slip lead

A slip lead is quick to put on and handy for moving dogs between the house, the car and the field. Used well, it sits high on the neck and gives a clear, light signal. We teach owners to keep it loose and to release the moment the dog responds, never to hang on with steady pressure.

Prong collar

A prong collar spreads pressure evenly around the neck instead of on one spot at the front, which many strong dogs respond to with far less effort than a flat collar. Fit is everything: it should sit snug and high, not dangle low. We only use one once the dog understands leash pressure, and we show owners how to use leash pressure as guidance, a bump for direction, or a leash pop when the work calls for a correction.

E-collar

We think of a modern e-collar as a communication tool, never a way to hurt or frighten a dog. Every dog starts by simply wearing the collar during meals, play and training until it feels normal. Only then do we look for the dog's working level: the lowest level the dog notices and responds to, where it shows a small sign of noticing, like an ear flick or a turn of the head. On a 0 to 100 dial, most dogs in a quiet setting work somewhere from the low single digits into the teens. It varies by dog, by collar fit and by how exciting the surroundings are, so we watch the dog, not the number. Ryan tests every collar on himself first.

At that level the sensation is more like a tap on the shoulder than anything sharp. We pair it with commands the dog already knows, the tap stops the moment the dog responds, and the response is always rewarded. Over time the dog learns that the collar simply means "listen, there's something good in this for you." The collar is never used to zap a dog for ordinary misbehaving.

In untrained hands, however good the intentions, an e-collar can hurt a dog physically and mentally. That is why we ask clients to keep their own e-collar work to the low, reward-paired level we teach, and only once we have trained them to use it properly.

Quality matters too. The brands we trust and use are E-Collar Technologies (the Educator line), our first choice for its fine 0 to 100 control and smooth, gentle stimulation, and Dogtra, our other recommendation. Cheap collars often have uneven output and big jumps between levels, which makes low-level work much harder.

For comparison, many trainers once recommended the rattle can: a soda can filled with pennies or stones, shaken or even thrown at a dog to startle it away from the counter or the garbage. Few people thought twice about it. A well-conditioned e-collar at a low level is far quieter and more precise than that, which is why we believe many calls to ban e-collars come from a misunderstanding of how modern trainers actually use them. For more, read our article on e-collar myths.

Muzzle

Every dog should be comfortable in a basket muzzle. It is a safety tool for vet visits, grooming and emergencies, not a sign of a bad dog. We teach it with food and patience so the dog pushes its own nose in. Our muzzle training article walks through the steps.

Long line

A long line gives a dog freedom while we keep a safe connection. It is how we build a recall in open spaces before a dog has earned time off leash, and it keeps the dog from learning that running off works.

Crate

A crate is a calm, safe place to rest, not a punishment. Paired with meals, chews and quiet, it helps with house training, travel and settling. See crate training for every dog.

Fair and in proportion

Whatever the tool, the rules are the same. Teach before you ask, reward the right choice, keep any pressure as light as the dog needs, and stop the moment the dog gets it. In the end, it all comes back to the dog. A well-trained dog lives a fuller, happier life because of the freedom training brings: more time off leash, more trust from the people around them, and more places they can go with their family.
