Why Place Training Matters for Every Dog

By Ryan R. McKenzie, Founder and Head Trainer, Acadia Canine Academy

If we could teach every dog only one thing beyond the basics, place would be near the top of the list. Place means going to a set spot, usually a raised cot or a mat, and staying there calmly until released. It sounds simple, and it is. That is exactly why it is so useful.

What place does for your dog

A dog that knows place has somewhere to be when the house gets busy. Instead of jumping on guests at the door, circling the table at dinner or following you from room to room, your dog has a job: go to the cot and relax.

Place also teaches a dog to settle. Many dogs that seem wild are not bad dogs. They have simply never practised switching off. Holding a place for a few minutes, then longer, builds the habit of calm. Over time, many dogs start choosing their place on their own when they want a rest.

For owners, place is a practical tool. It gives you a way to manage your dog without shutting it away in another room or holding it by the collar.

Why a raised cot works well

A raised dog cot has clear edges, so it is easy for a dog to understand where place begins and ends. It is also easy to move, which matters later when you want your dog to hold a place in a new room, on the porch or out in the yard. A mat or folded blanket can work too, but a cot tends to make the lesson clearer.

How to teach place

Keep your dog on a leash for the first sessions so you can guide it calmly.

  1. Walk your dog up to the cot and let it step on. As soon as all four feet are on, mark it with "yes" and reward on the cot.
  2. Add your word, such as "place", just before your dog steps on. Repeat until your dog moves onto the cot as soon as it hears the word.
  3. Ask for a few seconds of staying, then release with a clear word like "free" and walk your dog off. The release word matters. Your dog should learn that place lasts until you say otherwise.
  4. Slowly build time. Five seconds, then ten, then thirty. Reward calmly while your dog stays on the cot.
  5. Add distance by stepping back one step at a time, then add small distractions like walking around or opening a door.

If your dog steps off before you release it, calmly guide it back on with the leash and try a shorter stay. Gentle leash pressure shows the way back, and it releases the moment your dog returns. No scolding is needed. You are simply showing your dog that the only way off the cot is your release word.

Calm, not frozen

The goal is a dog that lies down and relaxes, not one that sits stiffly waiting for the next cue. Once your dog holds place well, reward the signs of calm: lying down, resting its head, a slow breath out. Keep your own energy low. Quiet praise and slow rewards help a dog settle more than an excited "good dog!" does.

Where place helps most

  • Greeting guests at the door
  • Mealtimes, so your dog is not begging at the table
  • Busy family moments, like getting kids out the door in the morning
  • Calm time after exercise, so your dog learns to rest instead of looking for trouble
  • Visits to new places, once your dog can hold place on a portable cot

Common mistakes

  • Asking for too much time too soon. Build duration in small steps so your dog keeps succeeding.
  • Forgetting the release. If your dog wanders off on its own and nothing happens, it learns that place ends whenever it likes.
  • Using place as punishment. Your dog's cot should be a good spot, not a time-out. Feed treats there and let it be a place your dog likes.
  • Only practising when things are already chaotic. Teach place when life is quiet, then use it when life gets busy.

Place and the bigger picture

Place fits naturally alongside the rest of your dog's training. Puppies can start learning to settle on a cot early in Puppy Foundations, and holding a position with distractions is part of the work in Basic Obedience and Advanced Obedience. For dogs that struggle with excitement or reactivity at home, place is often one of the first tools we use in Behaviour Modification.
